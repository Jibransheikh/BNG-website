Exploring the Public Participation Bill 2025

By Adalia An'ya Policy Analysis 2025

The Public Participation Bill 2025 is one of the latest Bills passed by the parliament of Kenya and has the capacity to transition Kenya's civic engagement into a standardized statutory framework. By establishing institutional ownership, operationalizing a uniform baseline across national and county levels and mainstreaming digital channels, this legislation seeks to protect critical state frameworks from perpetual litigation while enhancing democratic inclusivity. However, critical institutional gaps exist that bring the realization of this bill into question — whether it empowers the ordinary citizen, or if it makes it easier to achieve compliance on paper.

The bill is co-sponsored by Rarieda Member of Parliament Otiende Amollo and MP Samuel Chepkonga. It awaits presidential assent and represents a major structural shift for Kenya by transitioning the public participation exercise into a standardized statutory process done on multiple channels. The bill operationalizes public participation principles through clause 3, which is anchored on Articles 1, 10, 35, 69, 118, 174, and 201 of the Constitution of Kenya 2010.

Institutional Responsibility

This bill introduces institutional responsibility through assigning "responsible authorities" and extends mandate, binding all responsible authorities and public including the National, Senate and County assemblies, state corporations, the Judiciary and Executive offices. These National and County public bodies are mandated to develop targeted guidelines, suiting their unique mandate. This is a shift from relying on a rigid, one-size-fits-all approach.

Moreover, clause 13 mandates responsible authorities to ensure access and inclusivity in conducting public participation and the opportunity to give views on the matter under consideration and to take measures to conduct the exercise in a language the participants understand where English and Kiswahili are not well known.

Multi-Channel Approach

Clause 5 legitimizes a multi-channel approach. Participation can be handled through written submissions, oral hearings, or digital platforms and online forums. It provides that the depth and duration of the process must match the actual complexity and socio-economic impact of the mandate in question.

The Public Participation Bill 2025 formalizes and integrates digital portals, written electronic submissions, virtual forums and WhatsApp tracking channels by validating online channels, elevating civic tech and establishing digital engagement as a legally binding method of governance. Even though this bill heavily regulates the process of gathering data, it fails to mandate a clear structural feedback loop, neither does it force an institution to publish a justification matrix demonstrating why certain citizen proposals were accepted or rejected. Without a mandatory feedback loop, the door remains wide open for superficial exercises where citizen voices are officially logged but completely ignored.

A Legally Secure Roadmap

This Bill seeks to provide a rigid, legally secure operational roadmap, protecting future national frameworks from systemic litigation. Historically, initiatives and fiscal policies have been frozen by courts due to flawed public participation processes. Formalizing digital portals and electronic registers modernizes public participation to match Kenya's advancing digital landscape, curing the disparities between different counties by enforcing a uniform baseline of public inclusion nationwide and empowering specific public organs to create targeted public engagement frameworks that fit their unique mandates.

The Controversial Removal of Accountability

The most controversial changes in the amendments made during the final parliamentary review and approval stages was the removal of strict individual accountability metrics. Early drafts proposed a personal criminal fine of up to KSh 500,000 for responsible authorities who willfully ignored public inputs. This punitive measure was dropped in favor of gentle institutional compliance.

Parliament stripped the proposed legislation of its teeth, making enforcement heavily dependent on bureaucratic goodwill. The ultimate success of this soon-to-be legislation rests on civil society's ability to police its implementation because Parliament removed direct personal penalties for non-compliant officials, exposing the bill to the risk of institutional cynicism where institutional authorities can easily transform the framework into a sterile administrative checklist — holding empty forums and logging electronic data purely to fulfill the letter of the law while totally violating its democratic spirit.

From Passive Participants to Active Auditors

To ensure citizen engagement does not become a hollow bureaucratic process, Kenyans must transition from passive participants to active auditors to ensure that their voice is actively integrated into the laws of the land. This can be achieved by demanding public institutions to justify their decisions, proving that the voices of the people are not just archived, but actually heard, embodying the principles of Articles 10, 35 and 232 of the Constitution of Kenya 2010.

Operationalizing Your Civic Action

To operationalize your civic action, you can utilize clause 12 of the bill by adding a formal closing clause to all policy memoranda demanding that public bodies publish a transparent post-consultation feedback matrix detailing which citizen ideas were accepted or rejected. You can also submit an access-to-information letter under Clause 16 to your County Assembly Clerk and MCA to audit the draft local guidelines. This process ensures your county locks in dedicated funding for local language translations, sign language interpreters, and digital tracking infrastructures rather than quietly diluting its constitutional obligations.

Conclusion

Finally, the Public Participation Bill 2025 is a significant step forward to formalize citizen engagement enshrined in Articles 10 and 118 of the Constitution of Kenya 2010. It designs a statutory pathway for civic expression and participation while expanding digital channels and increasing outreach. It brings about an enforcement deficit and leaves room for a digital trust gap because it lacks robust measures to verify inputs and prevent automated submissions from distorting genuine public feedback. This can be achieved through integration of public feedback systems with secure valid identification platforms, while maintaining proper mechanisms of data protection. Kenyans must then utilize these spaces and tools to ensure the citizen's voice translates directly into legislative outcomes.
